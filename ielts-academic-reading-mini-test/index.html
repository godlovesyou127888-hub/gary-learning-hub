Reading Passage: The Return of Urban Wetlands

Read the passage and answer Questions 1–10. All text and questions are original practice material, not Cambridge IELTS examination content.

AFor much of the twentieth century, city planners viewed wetlands as wasted land. Marshes were drained to create space for roads, factories and housing. Yet many cities are now reversing that approach. They are restoring small areas of wetland beside rivers and in former industrial districts, hoping to manage flooding while creating spaces for wildlife.
BUnlike concrete channels, which move water quickly downstream, wetlands can temporarily store rainwater. Reeds and water-loving plants slow the movement of water, while soil absorbs some of it. This does not mean wetlands can prevent every flood. During an unusually intense storm, their capacity may be exceeded. Engineers therefore regard them as one part of a wider drainage system rather than a complete replacement for conventional infrastructure.
CRestoration also presents difficult choices. In one coastal city, planners wanted to excavate contaminated ground before allowing water to return. Removing the soil would be expensive, but leaving it undisturbed could expose wildlife to industrial pollutants. The project team carried out tests and eventually removed the most polluted material while treating other areas on site. Local residents were invited to review the plans, although their suggestions did not all become policy.
DResearchers are interested in benefits that are harder to measure than flood storage. Some urban wetlands attract migratory birds, and nearby walking paths may encourage people to spend time outdoors. However, a rise in visitor numbers does not by itself demonstrate improved mental health. Studies must separate the effect of visiting wetlands from other factors, including exercise, income and access to green spaces elsewhere.
EMonitoring is essential because restored wetlands can change considerably over time. Young plants may fail during dry summers, and invasive species can displace native vegetation. Rather than judging a project only at its opening ceremony, ecologists recommend collecting data over several years. This longer perspective can show whether flood protection and biodiversity gains continue after the initial investment.
